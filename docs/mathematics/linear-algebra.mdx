## Definition

### Vector spaces

<Definition title="Vector space">
A vector space over a field $F$ is an abelian group $(V, +, 0)$ together with a scalar
multiplication $F \times V \to V$ satisfying
$$
\alpha(x + y) = \alpha x + \alpha y, \quad (\alpha + \beta)x = \alpha x + \beta x, \quad
(\alpha\beta)x = \alpha(\beta x), \quad 1x = x.
$$
A map $T : V \to W$ is *linear* when $T(\alpha x + y) = \alpha T(x) + T(y)$.
</Definition>

Two derived notions are used constantly. A *basis* is a linearly independent spanning set; every
finite-dimensional space has one, and all bases have the same cardinality, the *dimension*. A
*subspace* is a subset closed under the operations, and the two subspaces attached to a linear map
are its *range* $\operatorname{ran}(T) = \{Tx : x \in V\}$ and its *kernel*
$\ker(T) = \{x : Tx = 0\}$, related by the rank-nullity theorem

$$
\dim \operatorname{ran}(T) + \dim \ker(T) = \dim V .
$$

#### Matrices as representations of maps

Once bases are fixed, a linear map $T : F^n \to F^m$ is represented by a unique matrix
$A \in F^{m \times n}$ with $T(x) = Ax$, and composition of maps corresponds to matrix
multiplication. The distinction between the map and the matrix is not pedantic in computation: an
operator may be applicable without any matrix existing in memory, and most large-scale solvers in
[Scientific Computing](/docs/scientific-computing) exploit exactly that. The entry
[Conjugate Gradient](/docs/examples/conjugate-gradient) is built on the distinction.

#### Change of basis and similarity

A change of basis by an invertible $P$ transforms $A$ into $P^{-1}AP$. Quantities invariant under
this transformation, namely the eigenvalues, the trace, the determinant and the characteristic
polynomial, are properties of the map. Quantities that are not invariant, notably the entries and
the condition number in a fixed norm, are properties of the representation. Numerical methods
that improve accuracy by rescaling are exploiting precisely this difference.

### Inner products and norms

<Definition title="Inner product space">
An inner product on a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ is a map
$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : V \times V \to F$ that is linear in its second argument,
conjugate-symmetric, and positive definite. It induces the norm
$\lVert x \rVert = \sqrt{\langle x, x \rangle}$.
</Definition>

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, $|\langle x, y\rangle| \le \lVert x\rVert \lVert y\rVert$, is what
makes angles definable, and it is the source of most estimates in Krylov theory.

#### Vector and matrix norms

The norms used in this reference:

$$
\lVert x \rVert_1 = \sum_i |x_i|, \qquad
\lVert x \rVert_2 = \Big(\sum_i |x_i|^2\Big)^{1/2}, \qquad
\lVert x \rVert_\infty = \max_i |x_i|,
$$

and the induced matrix norms, together with the Frobenius norm:

$$
\lVert A \rVert_1 = \max_j \sum_i |a_{ij}|, \quad
\lVert A \rVert_\infty = \max_i \sum_j |a_{ij}|, \quad
\lVert A \rVert_2 = \sigma_1, \quad
\lVert A \rVert_F = \Big( \sum_{i,j} |a_{ij}|^2 \Big)^{1/2}.
$$

Induced norms are submultiplicative, $\lVert AB \rVert \le \lVert A \rVert \lVert B \rVert$, and
every error analysis in this reference relies on that inequality. The $1$- and $\infty$-norms are
computable in $\Theta(mn)$ time; the $2$-norm requires the largest singular value and is therefore
estimated rather than computed in practice.

### Orthogonality

A set is orthonormal when $\langle q_i, q_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$. Orthonormal bases are the
numerically preferred representation of a subspace because the associated matrix $Q$ satisfies
$\lVert Qx \rVert_2 = \lVert x \rVert_2$, so applying $Q$ neither amplifies nor damps error. This
single property is why orthogonal transformations dominate stable numerical linear algebra:
Householder reflections and Givens rotations are used in place of the algebraically simpler
elimination steps precisely because they preserve the $2$-norm.

## Motivation

### Why linear algebra is the computational core

Most large computations are either linear or are solved by a sequence of linear problems.
Newton's method solves $J\delta = -r$ at each step. An implicit time integrator solves a linear
system per step. A discretised elliptic partial differential equation is a single large sparse
system. Least squares, eigenvalue problems and low-rank approximation are the standard tools of
data analysis.

### Why it is the performance core

Matrix multiplication has arithmetic intensity growing with the blocking factor, which makes it
the one dense kernel able to reach a large fraction of peak floating-point throughput. Algorithms
are therefore reformulated to spend their time in matrix-matrix products, that is, in blocked
factorizations rather than unblocked ones, and the reformulation is a linear-algebraic exercise
before it is a performance one. The quantitative version of the argument is in
[Matrix Multiplication](/docs/examples/matrix-multiplication).

### The three levels

The BLAS classification is a statement about arithmetic intensity, not about interfaces:

| Level | Operation | Data | Flops | Intensity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $y \leftarrow \alpha x + y$ | $\Theta(n)$ | $\Theta(n)$ | $\Theta(1)$ |
| 2 | $y \leftarrow \alpha Ax + \beta y$ | $\Theta(n^2)$ | $\Theta(n^2)$ | $\Theta(1)$ |
| 3 | $C \leftarrow \alpha AB + \beta C$ | $\Theta(n^2)$ | $\Theta(n^3)$ | $\Theta(n)$ |

Only level 3 has intensity growing with the problem size, and only level 3 can be compute-bound.
Every performance decision in dense linear algebra follows from this table.

## Formal formulation

### Spectra

The *spectrum* is $\sigma(A) = \{\lambda : \det(A - \lambda I) = 0\}$, with spectral radius
$\rho(A) = \max_{\lambda \in \sigma(A)} |\lambda|$. It determines the convergence of stationary
iterations.

<Proposition title="Convergence of stationary iterations">
The iteration $x_{k+1} = Mx_k + c$ converges to the unique fixed point for every starting vector
if and only if $\rho(M) < 1$, and the asymptotic rate is $\rho(M)$ per step.
</Proposition>

For a symmetric matrix the spectral theorem gives $A = Q\Lambda Q^{\mathsf{T}}$ with $Q$
orthogonal and $\Lambda$ real diagonal, which is why symmetric problems admit stronger algorithms
and sharper bounds than general ones.

### Singular values

The singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_n \ge 0$ of $A$ are the square roots of the
eigenvalues of $A^{*}A$, and $A = U\Sigma V^{*}$ is the singular value decomposition.

<Theorem title="Eckart and Young">
For any $k$, the best rank-$k$ approximation to $A$ in the $2$-norm and in the Frobenius norm is
obtained by truncating the singular value decomposition, and
$$
\min_{\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k} \lVert A - B \rVert_2 = \sigma_{k+1}, \qquad
\min_{\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k} \lVert A - B \rVert_F = \Big(\sum_{j>k}\sigma_j^2\Big)^{1/2}.
$$
</Theorem>

This theorem justifies every low-rank method: it says that the singular value spectrum, not the
sparsity pattern or the size, determines how far a matrix can be compressed.

### Conditioning

<Definition title="Condition number">
For invertible $A$, $\kappa_p(A) = \lVert A \rVert_p \lVert A^{-1} \rVert_p$, with
$\kappa_2(A) = \sigma_1/\sigma_n$.
</Definition>

<Proposition title="Sensitivity of a linear system">
If $(A + \delta A)(x + \delta x) = b + \delta b$ and
$\lVert \delta A\rVert \lVert A^{-1}\rVert < 1$, then
$$
\frac{\lVert \delta x \rVert}{\lVert x \rVert} \le
\frac{\kappa(A)}{1 - \kappa(A)\lVert \delta A\rVert/\lVert A\rVert}
\left( \frac{\lVert \delta A \rVert}{\lVert A \rVert} + \frac{\lVert \delta b \rVert}{\lVert b \rVert} \right).
$$
</Proposition>

The practical reading is a rule of thumb: with $\kappa_2(A) \approx 10^{k}$ in binary64, roughly
$16 - k$ significant decimal digits survive. At $\kappa_2 = 10^{16}$ nothing survives, and the
computed solution carries no information about the exact one, whatever algorithm produced it.

### Positive definiteness

$A$ is symmetric positive definite when $A = A^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $x^{\mathsf{T}}Ax > 0$ for all
$x \neq 0$. Equivalent characterisations, each used somewhere in this reference: all eigenvalues
positive; all leading principal minors positive; a Cholesky factorization exists with positive
diagonal. The last is the only one cheap to test, and it is a test by attempted computation
rather than by inspection.

Positive definiteness licenses two things. It makes $\langle x, y\rangle_A = x^{\mathsf{T}}Ay$ an
inner product, which is what conjugate gradient minimises in. And it removes the need for
pivoting in Cholesky factorization, which halves the arithmetic and removes data-dependent
control flow from the inner loop.

## C++ representation

### Spaces and operators as concepts

```cpp
#include <concepts>
#include <cstddef>

namespace hpc {

template <class V, class F>
concept VectorSpace =
    std::regular<V> &&
    Field<F> &&
    requires(V x, V y, F a) {
        { x + y } -> std::same_as<V>;
        { x - y } -> std::same_as<V>;
        { a * x } -> std::same_as<V>;
        { V{}   } -> std::same_as<V>;
    };

template <class V, class F>
concept InnerProductSpace =
    VectorSpace<V, F> &&
    requires(const V& x, const V& y) {
        { inner_product(x, y) } -> std::same_as<F>;
        { norm(x) }             -> std::convertible_to<F>;
    };

// A linear operator need not be a matrix: only its action is required.
template <class A, class V>
concept LinearOperator = requires(const A& op, const V& x, V& y) {
    { op.rows() } -> std::convertible_to<std::size_t>;
    { op.cols() } -> std::convertible_to<std::size_t>;
    op.apply(x, y);                 // y <- A x
};

template <class A, class V>
concept SelfAdjointOperator = LinearOperator<A, V>;
// Semantic requirement: <Ax, y> == <x, Ay> for all x, y.

}  // namespace hpc
```

#### Why the operator concept is the load-bearing one

`LinearOperator` admits a dense matrix, a sparse matrix, a finite-difference stencil applied
without assembly, a factored preconditioner, and a Gram operator $B^{\mathsf{T}}B$ applied as two
products without forming the product. The last is the case that justifies the abstraction:
forming $B^{\mathsf{T}}B$ costs $\Theta(mn^2)$ and squares the condition number, whereas applying
it costs $\Theta(mn)$ and does neither.

#### Semantic obligations

```cpp
// Semantic requirements for VectorSpace<V, F>:
//   S1. (V, +) is an abelian group.
//   S2. Scalar multiplication distributes over both additions.
//   S3. 1 * x == x.
//
// For InnerProductSpace, additionally:
//   S4. <x, y> is linear in its second argument and conjugate-symmetric.
//   S5. <x, x> > 0 for x != 0.
//   S6. norm(x) == sqrt(<x, x>).
//
// For SelfAdjointOperator, additionally:
//   S7. <Ax, y> == <x, Ay>.
//   S8. apply is a pure function of its input.
```

S1 to S3 fail for floating-point vectors by the bounds of
[Numerical Analysis](/docs/mathematics/numerical-analysis). S7 is the obligation that conjugate
gradient depends on and that no compiler checks, which is why the implementation there tests the
curvature it computes rather than trusting the caller.

### Dimensions as types or as values

Fixed extents make conformability a deduction problem; dynamic extents make it a precondition:

```cpp
#include <cstddef>
#include <mdspan>

namespace hpc {

// Static extents: a non-conforming product fails to deduce.
template <class T, std::size_t M, std::size_t K, std::size_t N>
constexpr auto multiply(std::mdspan<const T, std::extents<std::size_t, M, K>> A,
                        std::mdspan<const T, std::extents<std::size_t, K, N>> B)
    -> std::mdspan<T, std::extents<std::size_t, M, N>>;

// Dynamic extents: conformability is a documented precondition.
// Requires: A.extent(1) == B.extent(0).
void multiply(std::mdspan<const double, std::dextents<std::size_t, 2>> A,
              std::mdspan<const double, std::dextents<std::size_t, 2>> B,
              std::mdspan<double, std::dextents<std::size_t, 2>> C) noexcept;

}  // namespace hpc
```

The static form is right for small fixed shapes, where it also unlocks full unrolling; the
dynamic form is right above a few tens, where an instantiation per shape would dominate build
time and instruction cache. The rule and its justification are in
[Type-Level Programming](/docs/formal-cpp/type-level-programming).

## Implementation

### Conjugate gradient, written against the operator

The canonical demonstration that the abstraction is the right one:

```cpp
#include <cmath>
#include <cstddef>
#include <span>

namespace hpc {

struct cg_result {
    std::size_t iterations;
    double residual_norm;
};

// Solves A x = b for symmetric positive-definite A. The operator is used only
// through apply(); no representation of A is assumed.
template <class Operator>
cg_result conjugate_gradient(const Operator& A,
                             std::span<const double> b,
                             std::span<double> x,
                             std::span<double> r,
                             std::span<double> p,
                             std::span<double> Ap,
                             double tolerance,
                             std::size_t max_iterations)
{
    A.apply(x, Ap);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < b.size(); ++i) {
        r[i] = b[i] - Ap[i];
        p[i] = r[i];
    }

    double rr = inner_product(r, r);
    const double stop = tolerance * tolerance * rr;

    for (std::size_t k = 0; k < max_iterations; ++k) {
        if (rr <= stop) { return {k, std::sqrt(rr)}; }

        A.apply(p, Ap);
        const double alpha = rr / inner_product(p, Ap);

        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < b.size(); ++i) {
            x[i] += alpha * p[i];
            r[i] -= alpha * Ap[i];
        }

        const double rr_next = inner_product(r, r);
        const double beta = rr_next / rr;
        rr = rr_next;

        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < b.size(); ++i) {
            p[i] = r[i] + beta * p[i];
        }
    }
    return {max_iterations, std::sqrt(rr)};
}

}  // namespace hpc
```

The workspace is supplied by the caller rather than allocated, which keeps the routine usable
from a context with a fixed memory budget and makes the $\Theta(n)$ working set explicit in the
signature.

### Orthogonalisation, and why the obvious form is wrong

Classical Gram-Schmidt is algebraically correct and numerically useless: the computed vectors lose
orthogonality at a rate proportional to $\kappa_2(A)^2$. The modified form loses it at a rate
proportional to $u\,\kappa_2(A)$, and Householder reflections do not lose it to first order.

```cpp
#include <cmath>
#include <cstddef>
#include <span>

namespace hpc {

// Modified Gram-Schmidt: each new vector is orthogonalised against the
// already-orthogonalised basis one vector at a time, using the updated vector
// for each projection. Loss of orthogonality is O(u kappa) rather than
// O(u kappa^2).
inline void modified_gram_schmidt(std::span<std::span<double>> columns) {
    for (std::size_t j = 0; j < columns.size(); ++j) {
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < j; ++i) {
            const double proj = inner_product(columns[i], columns[j]);
            for (std::size_t k = 0; k < columns[j].size(); ++k) {
                columns[j][k] -= proj * columns[i][k];
            }
        }
        const double nrm = std::sqrt(inner_product(columns[j], columns[j]));
        for (double& v : columns[j]) { v /= nrm; }
    }
}

}  // namespace hpc
```

The difference between the two variants is which vector is used to compute the projection, and it
changes the attainable accuracy by a factor of $\kappa$. This is the standard illustration that
algebraic equivalence does not imply numerical equivalence.

### Estimating a condition number

$\kappa_2$ requires the smallest singular value and is therefore not computed directly in
practice. The standard substitute estimates $\lVert A^{-1}\rVert_1$ with a few solves against an
existing factorization:

```cpp
namespace hpc {

// Hager and Higham 1-norm estimator: about five triangular solves against an
// existing LU factorization, giving a lower bound on ||A^{-1}||_1 that is
// within a small factor in practice.
double estimate_condition_1(const auto& lu_factorization);

}  // namespace hpc
```

An estimate that is occasionally too small by a factor of two is adequate, because the quantity
is used to decide how many digits to trust, and that decision is made on an order of magnitude.

## Performance considerations

### Arithmetic intensity organises everything

The distinction that organises dense linear algebra is operations per byte moved. Level-1 and
level-2 operations have intensity $O(1)$ and are bandwidth-bound; level-3 has intensity
$\Theta(b)$ for a blocking factor $b$ and is the only level that can approach peak.

The consequence is architectural rather than local. An algorithm is restructured so that its
arithmetic occurs in level-3 operations: blocked LU performs the same $\tfrac{2}{3}n^3$ operations
as the unblocked form and runs an order of magnitude faster, because those operations occur inside
matrix-matrix products on cache-resident blocks. The transfer-count analysis is in
[Cache Locality](/docs/high-performance/cache-locality).

### Iterative methods invert the picture

For sparse iterative methods the operator application is bandwidth-bound and irreducibly so. The
available levers are the storage format, the ordering of the unknowns, and the preconditioner,
which trades operator applications for a better $\kappa$ and is therefore an algorithmic rather
than a hardware optimisation. A preconditioner that halves the iteration count and triples the
cost per iteration is a loss, and only the product distinguishes them.

### Conditioning bounds what any implementation can deliver

If $\kappa_2(A) \approx 10^{12}$ in binary64, roughly four significant digits survive, and no
arrangement of the arithmetic recovers them. The responses are reformulation, scaling, or
extended precision, not tuning. Iterative refinement in a higher precision is the cheapest of
these and is developed in
[Numerical Linear Algebra](/docs/scientific-computing/numerical-linear-algebra).

### Symmetry is worth exploiting twice

Exploiting symmetry halves both the storage and the arithmetic, and it also removes pivoting from
the factorization, which removes data-dependent control flow from the inner loop. The combination
is usually worth more than the factor of two that the operation count alone suggests.

## Limitations

Concepts state the operations of a vector space and cannot state its axioms. `VectorSpace<V, F>`
is satisfied by types whose addition is not associative, which includes every floating-point
vector type in use.

The `LinearOperator` concept cannot state that an operator is symmetric or positive definite.
Conjugate gradient applied to an operator that is neither may converge to something, diverge, or
stall, without any diagnostic. Where the property is essential it is checked probabilistically at
debug level, by testing $\langle Ax, y \rangle \approx \langle x, Ay \rangle$ for random
$x, y$, and documented as a precondition.

Dimensions are runtime values in the dynamic interface, so conformability is a precondition
rather than a type error. Fixed-extent `mdspan` moves the check to translation time at the cost of
an instantiation per shape.

The $2$-norm and the condition number in it are not computable at the cost of the operations they
qualify, so they are estimated. Estimates are lower bounds, and an estimator that under-reports by
a factor of two is normal.
